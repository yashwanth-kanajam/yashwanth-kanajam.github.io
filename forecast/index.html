Demand Forecast Performance & Operational Exceptions

How reliable is an incumbent day-ahead demand forecast, where does it fail systematically, and which of those errors can actually be corrected?

Question

Balancing authorities publish an hourly day-ahead demand forecast alongside the demand that followed, through the U.S. Energy Information Administration's Form EIA-930. The forecast is the object being evaluated: the question is how it behaves, not whether a new model could beat it.

The same review applies to any forecast a team relies on but did not build, such as a sales forecast, a demand plan or a budget.

Evaluation framework

The data cover 36 complete months, September 2023 to August 2026, for eight balancing authorities. They were chosen for reporting quality and diversity from 50 that passed a 95% paired-coverage gate, before any error metric was computed.

Measures
MeasureUse
WAPEPrimary cross-authority comparison: total absolute error over total demand
MAE (MW)Error magnitude within one authority
Normalized biasDirection of error; negative means underforecast

Every forecast is compared with a seasonal-naive benchmark: the same reporting-clock hour seven days earlier. All comparisons use an identical set of hours. A large-miss threshold metric was tested and dropped because stable bias confounded it.

Bias correction

The corrected forecast adds the mean residual for the same authority and reporting hour over days D−29 to D−2. It uses only data that would have been available at the time, needs at least 14 days of history, and is never back-filled.

Tests perturb the target day, the previous day and day D−30 and confirm the correction does not change; perturbing D−2 and D−29 does change it. Improvement is judged by year and by quarter, never from a single aggregate.

Key finding

Forecast error was not equally correctable across systems. ISNE, PJM and SOCO showed persistent directional bias that the historical adjustment reduced in all 12 quarters. Persistent bias alone did not guarantee that result (see TVA below). Where the published forecast was already close to unbiased, the correction added nothing or made it slightly worse.

The incumbent forecasts are not weak. For six of the eight authorities, the published forecast removes 61–86% of the seasonal-naive benchmark's error. The correctable part is a stable directional bias, which aggregate WAPE alone would hide.

Where correction did not help

AECI and BPAT were already effectively unbiased, and the correction gave no real benefit (BPAT) or made WAPE slightly worse (AECI, 1 of 12 quarters improved). TVA is the counterexample: its bias was persistent, but the correction changed overall WAPE by only about 0.05 percentage points and improved 10 of 12 quarters.

An apparent PJM peak-hour underforecast was not reported as a finding. It appears when peaks are selected by actual demand, which is retrospective, and disappears when they are selected by forecast demand.

Source semantics

PNM and PSCO are marked SOURCE SEMANTICS UNCERTAIN. Their reported forecasts sit persistently below actual demand, by 5.8% and 9.4%, and the correction reduces that gap mathematically. Available EIA metadata does not resolve whether this reflects forecast-process bias, reporting-scope differences, resubmission behaviour or another source-definition effect.

Both are reported separately and are not used as evidence for the headline finding. A correctable number in the reported series is not proof that the underlying forecasting process failed.

Limitations

EIA revises values in place, and forecast vintages and resubmissions are not observable. Authorities were selected on reporting quality, which may favour operationally mature systems; Texas and California are not represented. Seasonal statements rest on three annual cycles. No cost, reliability or operational impact is estimated.
